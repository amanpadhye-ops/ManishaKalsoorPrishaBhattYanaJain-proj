Section D · Phase 1

Stakeholder Issues

Primary research with the organising team of TEDx FLAME University 2027 (theme “Wildfire – What Burns, Becomes”), mapping stakeholder needs, pain points and expectations in a live event setting.

NeedsPain pointsExpectationsConclusion

Research context

Event. TEDx FLAME University 2027.

Stakeholders interviewed. Heer Desai (Co-Chief, Curation), Akshita Joshi (Chief of Staff) and Dhwani Kothari (Chief of Staff, Brand & Technology). All three are organisers, and they also spoke about what speakers, sponsors, the audience, the university and TED need.

View supporting document on Google Drive

D1

Stakeholder needs

Organisers need three core resources.

1. Sponsorship funding

“Where our budget and everything comes from.”Akshita, on sponsors

This money pays for early-bird and themed events, PR, and speakers’ airfare, hotels and transport (Heer).

2. High-profile speakers

All three called speakers the main reason people attend TEDx. Getting them depends on a strong network of contacts (Dhwani).

3. Audience and community

Volunteers, members and word of mouth, as well as ticket buyers.

Needs of other stakeholders

Speakers

Hospitality, travel arrangements and invitations sent early, before their calendars fill up.

Sponsors

Visibility in proportion to their investment.

University and TED

The Office of Student Affairs and the Registrar must approve every activity. TEDx, as the licence holder, requires strict compliance with its rules.

Students and faculty

A lineup that interests them and a clear understanding of what TEDx is and why it is worth attending (Heer).

D2

Pain points

Late planning and heavy workload

In the past the speaker search only began after the theme was finalised, which caused big delays. Tasks were pushed to the last minute (Heer, Dhwani).

Getting speakers

TEDx does not allow paying speakers, and many turn down the invitation once they learn there is no fee (Dhwani).

Licence limits on sponsors

A sponsor’s branding cannot be larger than the TEDx logo. It is also awkward when someone wants to speak and have their company sponsor the event or run a stall (Heer, Dhwani).

Problems on event days

One speaker arrived late last year, which delayed the event, upset the schedule, frustrated the audience and unsettled the other speakers (Akshita).

Limited reach

Ticket sales stayed mostly within FLAME students, friends and family (Dhwani).

Layers of approval

Every activity needs sign-off from both the university and TEDx, which adds lead time (Heer).

D3

Expectations

A bigger audience

Larger than last year, with reach beyond campus (Heer, Akshita).

Keeping the prestige

Preserve the standing set by the previous edition, which students now see as something to aspire to (Dhwani).

Inspiring content

Well-known speakers whose talks fit the “Wildfire” theme and leave attendees inspired (Akshita).

A rewarding team experience

The curation team should enjoy their months of work and want to come back next year (Heer), and the team’s “family” feeling should continue (Dhwani).

Lasting recall

People should look forward to TEDx every year, the way they do for Kurukshetra (Dhwani).

Fair returns for sponsors

Promotion should match the sponsor’s tier (for example, ₹50,000 + GST at entry level) and stay within TED’s rules (Akshita).

A smooth event for speakers

Good hospitality and a schedule that runs on time.

D4

Conclusion and key findings

Coordination is the core challenge

The interviews reveal that event management is fundamentally dependent on coordinating multiple stakeholders with different expectations. Organisers must balance sponsorship requirements, speaker arrangements, audience engagement and institutional approvals, often simultaneously. A communication gap or delay involving one stakeholder can create a ripple effect across the entire event, affecting schedules, audience experience and overall execution.

Event marketing starts early

Another significant insight is that event marketing begins well before the event itself. Securing high-profile speakers, attracting sponsors and building audience interest require early outreach and sustained relationship-building. This is particularly important for TEDx, where speakers cannot be paid and organisers rely heavily on professional networks and the appeal of the event.

Success means different things

The interviews also highlight that different stakeholders measure event success differently. Sponsors expect visibility proportional to their investment, speakers expect recognition and hospitality, while attendees seek an engaging and inspiring experience. This makes stakeholder-specific communication essential.

The opportunity for IntelliConvene

For IntelliConvene, the findings highlight an opportunity to address fragmented communication and coordination across the event lifecycle. Organisers need a structured way to manage stakeholder relationships, share information and coordinate activities, while ensuring that each participant’s expectations are addressed.
